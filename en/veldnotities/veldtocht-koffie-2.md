<p class="lead">Joost and Lars were sitting in a cabin at the Huis van Actief Burgerschap in Utrecht for a conversation about Social AI. Before they recorded, they had already done a quick check-in. Joost switched Dembrane on. Lars's first question once the device was running: why not record the check-in too? What followed are six places where Social AI shows itself most sharply: a check-in, a rupture, a mirror, a paradox, a campfire and an echo.</p>

<section class="v-chapter" id="hoofdstuk-1">
<span class="phase-chip start"><span class="dot"></span>Chapter 01 · The check-in</span>

## "Why don't we record the check-in?"

<div class="en-title">The implicit promise: what shifts when the AI is listening in from minute one</div>

<p class="lead">It's the first question Lars asks the moment the recording starts. They had just checked in without a microphone. Lars notes: shouldn't that actually have been on tape too? And without anyone noticing, that question opens everything that comes after. What shifts when the tool is listening in from moment one?</p>

The check-in is the moment where you put on the table what you want to experience. In the past you carried that intention yourself. At the end you'd look at whether you had experienced it, and if not, there was apparently a good reason for that. <em class="term-forest">You were the owner of what you had wanted.</em>

But when you know it's being recorded, then you can, Lars says, "switch on the big rambler and just chatter away": let go, just talk, see what comes out. It's not a joke about the device, it's an inner stance. And that's exactly where the first shift sits, the one they keep circling for the whole hour.

<span class="phase-chip p2"><span class="dot"></span>Chapter 02 · The rupture</span>

## A session a while back.

<div class="en-title">The rupture, when an implicit procedure surfaces as anger</div>

<p class="lead">Joost tells about an earlier session. A large gathering. Dembrane in every conversation. Forty people, four breakouts. At the end the facilitator asks for a report-back. Someone gets angry.</p>

<em>"You introduced the AI as the one that would take the minutes. So we didn't appoint a chair. Didn't appoint a minute-taker. And now you're suddenly changing the way of working like this."</em>

It's a small incident, but it shows something big. The procedure these people have known their whole professional lives, <em class="term">appoint someone, keep time, give a summary</em>, had fallen away without anyone saying so. The AI would handle it.

## Turning the intuitive into instructions.

<div class="en-title">The mirror on the craft: putting into words what you used to only feel</div>

<p class="lead">Joost is working on something he provisionally calls the twelve principles of Social AI. He notices that the exercise of explaining to a machine what a good facilitator does forces him to articulate that craft more precisely than was ever needed in front of a person.</p>

How do you teach a Large Language Model that not every tension needs to be resolved? That <em>"there is no agreement"</em> is also a valid outcome? That an individual shouldn't just be put in the spotlight? That an AI can't damage a bond of trust, because it doesn't have one, but that such a bond does very much exist between the people taking part?

<span class="phase-chip p3"><span class="dot"></span>Chapter 04 · The paradox</span>

## A concept that came out of a series of steering-group sessions.

<div class="en-title">The bottom-up paradox: an impossibility you can only navigate</div>

<p class="lead">Joost reads something out loud. The concept emerged from a series of AI interactions on transcripts of the steering-group meetings in Doesburg. It has been given a name.</p>

<div class="v-concept" id="term-paradox">
<div class="label">Definition · Concept distilled from steering-group sessions in Doesburg</div>
<h3>The bottom-up paradox</h3>
<div class="term-class">noun, facilitation, organizational change</div>
<p>The felt tension that every form of steering, including the steering to work bottom-up, is by definition top-down.</p>
<p>The more structure you bring in as a facilitator to organize participation, the more the group tends to fill in that structure as if it were an assignment.</p>
<p>The paradox is inherent and unsolvable. <em>You can only navigate it consciously.</em></p>
</div>

Lars recognizes it immediately. <em>"Every bottom-up process starts with someone making a top-down decision that something bottom-up is going to happen."</em> And then a second paradox arises between them. One they had run into before, in a two-day session with Floor: does change begin with someone who holds a strong opinion, or does it begin with someone who listens?

<span class="phase-chip meta"><span class="dot"></span>Chapter 05 · The campfire</span>

## Do you program the program with the humanity?

<div class="en-title">The campfire: task versus relationship, and the human cost of efficiency</div>

<p class="lead">From under the tabletop comes the real concern. Lars names it: the AI is good at directedness. Joost puts it in one word: efficiency. And then what?</p>

Lars calls it his <em class="term" id="term-onmaskering">fear of unmasking</em>: the fear that a machine will give back, afterwards, how much irrationality, repetition and bullshit there is in your own hour. Joost finds it scary too. But underneath that fear lies something bigger, and it's Lars who brings it to the surface.

<em>Work isn't only about earning your bread.</em> It's nice to sit around a campfire and talk with each other. It's nice to throw a log on the fire and hold a bite for the other person. If there's an <em>abundance</em> of apple trees around it, work is mostly a social activity that we, as social beings, find pleasant.
